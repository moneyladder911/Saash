## 0. THE ANSWER, UP FRONT

**Is it worth showing investors? Yes — but not this deck, for this ask.**

The document is genuinely well made. Sourcing discipline is above average for the stage, the tariff work is exact, the factory costing is honest, and slide 14 states "No revenue yet. The risk is execution and demand" — which is more candour than most pre-revenue decks manage. Sending it out will get meetings.

It will not get a $20M term sheet, and the reason is not polish. **Three of its load-bearing claims do not survive contact with public information**, and a sophisticated investor will find at least one of them in the first meeting. Showing it as-is spends the founders' most valuable non-renewable asset — first impressions with a finite list of category investors — on a version that can be falsified in front of them.

| | |
|---|---|
| **P(company is a going concern & self-funding in 5 years)** | **~30%** |
| **P(venture-scale outcome, ≥3x)** | **~10%** |

**The price is what breaks, not the product.** That is the single most important sentence in this report, and it is also the good news: price is the one variable the founders fully control.

## 2. THE THREE FALSIFIABLE CLAIMS

These are ranked by how badly they damage credibility if an investor finds them first. All three were found by sealed agents using only public sources — which means any competent associate can find them.

### 2.1 🔴 Slide 8's central claim is false, and a brand named on the slide breaks it

> *"Across all seventeen, the first two columns are empty."*

Two agents, working independently and finding different evidence, both falsified this.

- **FOREO** — listed on slide 8 as a partial — has shipped the **BEAR / BEAR 2** since 2019, which measures the skin's electrical resistance 100–200×/second and auto-adjusts treatment intensity in 0.002s. That is column 1 *and* column 2. FOREO also runs a docking formulation line (BEAR serum, UFO masks) and 4+ device categories. **FOREO plausibly has all four capabilities today.** FOREO also shipped the **LUNA fofo** in 2018 — contact moisture sensors driving an app-personalised routine.
- **Nu Skin** — placed by the deck in the "none of the four" bucket — launched **Prysm iO** in Q4 2025: a hardware skin scanner taking 70,000+ hyperspectral absorption measurements per scan, feeding an AI score and product recommendations.
- **Amorepacific** — not among the seventeen at all — unveiled **Skinsight™** at CES 2026 (with MIT), an e-skin sensor patch feeding directly into its ONFACE LED Mask. It won a CES 2026 Innovation Award.
- Also absent from the seventeen: **L'Oréal Cell BioPrint** (CES 2025, proteomic lab-on-chip), **LG Pra.L, AMIRO, JOVS, Ulike, COSBEAUTY** — the Korean and Chinese houses where device/sensing convergence is furthest along.

**Why this is the most dangerous slide in the deck.** It is not an overstatement; it is a checkable factual claim, it is the foundation of slides 9 and 10, and the counter-example is a company the deck itself lists. An investor who checks FOREO's own product page during the meeting does not then re-open the margin discussion.

**And the deeper problem is worse than the error.** The columns aren't empty because nobody thought of it. They're empty because **the category tried this and retreated**:
- **Neutrogena killed the SkinScanner hardware** and relaunched Skin360 as camera-only in January 2020 — the exact inverse of SAASH's thesis, executed by the company that tried it first.
- **P&G dissolved Opté** in January 2023, a $599 device with a 20,000-person waitlist.
- **HiMirror** bricked every unit when its servers shut on 31 March 2024.
- **FOREO LUNA fofo** — discontinued. **Wayskin** (2016–19) — gone.

That history is the strongest argument *against* the thesis and the deck does not engage with it. Engaging with it head-on would be far more persuasive than the matrix.

### 2.2 🔴 The sebum sensor, as described, may not be physically possible

> *"Sensors in the controller measure hydration and sebum directly from the skin surface… validated against the Courage + Khazaka standard."*

- **Hydration** — real, and genuinely melanin-independent, because capacitance corneometry reads water in the stratum corneum and melanin sits in the basal epidermis. But it is a **commodity**: Shenzhen suppliers sell combined moisture/oil OEM probe modules for tens of dollars, and the Corneometer principle is 35+ years old.
- **Sebum — this is the finding.** The **Sebumeter SM 815 is not a sensor.** It is *grease-spot photometry*: a consumable matte tape cartridge (~400 measurements) pressed to the skin and read by a photocell. There is no established solid-state contact sebum sensor. Every credible alternative requires a collection medium — Oil Red O film, or a microbalance.

**So one of two things is true, and the deck must say which:** either the sebum reading is a reflectance proxy being described as corneometry-grade measurement, or the device ships a consumable — in which case **there is a consumable cost line missing from slide 12** (and, incidentally, the recurring-revenue problem in §2.3 partly solves itself).

Two further defects in the same claim: **"the Courage + Khazaka standard" is factually wrong** — the Corneometer® and Sebumeter® are trademarked instruments, not a standard; calling them "the German standard" is an unsubstantiated establishment claim and a trademark-use issue. And **the melanin-independence argument is undercut on its own slide**: slide 9 keeps a camera path for redness, hyperpigmentation and texture — which is precisely where documented skin-tone bias lives. The pigmentation channel, the most melanin-sensitive of all, is on the camera.

**The crux nobody has evidence for:** no clinical trial exists anywhere testing LED protocols adapted to hydration or sebum readings. Worse, the best-powered relevant RCT (137 women, phototypes II–IV, split-face, 2023) found LED reduced periocular wrinkle volume ~30% but **did not improve hydration at all**. The measured variable is one the modality has not been shown to move. On current evidence the personalisation is a strong *merchandising and retention* mechanic — which is a legitimate thing to build — but the deck sells it as clinical, and slide 10 rests the entire moat on it.

### 2.3 🔴 Slide 11 assumes a 100% attach rate

This is the load-bearing fabrication, and it is subtler than the CAC objection everyone reaches for first.

Slide 11 presents "THE CUSTOMER SO FAR — $960, net +$512" as though the second order is a property of the model. It is an assumption, it is never labelled, and it is set to **100%**.

Category evidence:
- Health & beauty leads all verticals at 21.5% first-to-second purchase — but **77% of second purchases are reorders of the same product; only 23% are cross-sells.** A durable device cannot be reordered, so 77% of the normal repeat mechanism does not exist here. 21.5% × 23% ≈ **4.9%**.
- Durables: above 15% repeat is *outperforming*. Luxury 12-month repeat ≈ 10%.
- The step is 4.6× ($170 → $790), across a demographic break (acne/blue-light buyer → anti-ageing mask buyer). Both cut it further.
- **No disclosed device-to-device attach rate exists for any at-home beauty brand.** Panel estimate: **3% low / 8% base / 15% high** over 24 months.

**"Zero acquisition cost on order two" is also false.** Retention marketing runs ~$0.08 per revenue dollar — cheaper than acquisition, not free — plus promo, since $790 devices move on discount. Net contribution per attach is ~$347, not $489.

**Rebuilt cohort, 1,000 customers, 24 months, at $140 CAC and 8% attach: LTV $134 → LTV/CAC 0.96x.** The cohort loses money. To reach 3:1 requires **~91% attach**. Even at the deck's own $80 CAC, 3:1 needs ~40% attach — higher than the best repeat rate any *consumables* beauty brand posts.

## 3. WHERE THE DECK IS RIGHT (and the earlier read was wrong)

Adversarial review that only finds faults is not diligence. Three corrections, including to the critique that prompted this panel:

### 3.1 ✅ The $135 ex-factory cost is plausible — arguably conservative

The intuition that "2,000-unit minimums should produce worse unit costs" is reasonable but **wrong here**. Independent bottom-up BOM at 2,000 units: 213 tri-die packages $13–26, rigid-flex mask PCB $12–22, PCBA labour $8–15, medical silicone $8–14, controller $7–12, sensing front end $6–15, battery/USB-C $4–7, packaging $5–10, assembly/QC $6–12 → **$72–139 subtotal, $85–155 ex-factory after factory margin.** $135 sits upper-middle. They are quoting their *worst* cost point, not a flattering one.

*Caveat:* $135 almost certainly excludes tooling/NRE ($30–80k → $15–40/unit at 2k) and certification testing ($25–75/unit at 2k). True first-run cost is nearer **$150–175**, and contribution is ~10% overstated at launch volume ($436 vs $489). At 20k units the deck's numbers become roughly right.

### 3.2 ✅ The 40.1% tariff decomposes exactly

2.6% MFN on HTS 8543.70.96 (correct classification — LED masks fall there, not 9018, per CBP N246248) + 25% Section 301 List 3 + 12.5% Section 301 forced-labor effective 24 July 2026. **This is careful, current, verifiable work** and it is the single most credibility-earning detail in the deck.

*But:* that 12.5% component was 27 days old at the date of review and is already under challenge — 25 state AGs filed at the CIT on 3 August 2026. Three tariff statutes in eighteen months (IEEPA struck down Feb 2026 → Section 122 bridge → Section 301). Meanwhile **"margins that survive a 40% tariff" is true but trivially so** — ex-factory is 17% of retail, so every 10 points of duty costs only 1.7 points of gross margin. Tariff was never this company's risk, and claiming it as a strength invites a stress-test the company will pass while drawing attention away from the ones it won't.

### 3.3 ✅ The margin gap has an honest answer — the deck just doesn't give it

"78–83% vs incumbents at 62.7%" is not explained by "we're DTC": **TBTG is >90% e-commerce DTC and still only 62.7%.** And BeautyHealth's 65.3% is a category error as a comp — that's capital equipment sold wholesale to clinics plus consumables.

The real answer: **SAASH prices at $790 a device that costs roughly what a $395–650 device costs.** That is a *demand bet, not a margin achievement.* Stating it that way is both honest and stronger, because it converts an unanswerable "why are you better than listed companies?" into an answerable "here is why this device commands a premium." The deck currently asserts the margin and invites the wrong question.

**One internal-audit note in the deck's favour:** every figure on slides 11 and 12 ties. Contribution reconciles to gross margin, US-to-UAE deltas reconcile to duty differences, the +$23/+$37/+$489/+$549 all recompute exactly. **The arithmetic is clean; the inputs are the problem.** That distinction matters — it means this is a company with an assumptions problem, not a rigour problem, and assumptions are fixable in ninety days.

## 4. THE REGULATORY CALENDAR IS OFF BY NINE MONTHS

Not the budget — the calendar. The ~$2M bucket is arguably adequate; the dates are not.

**Verification of the deck's own citations, and a problem inside it:** K242796 (SharkNinja CryoGlow, cleared 16 Sep 2024) and K242593 (CurrentBody 4-in-1 Zone Mask, cleared 14 Feb 2025) both check out. But **the applicant of record on K242593 is Shenzhen Kaiyan Medical Equipment Co., not CurrentBody.** In this category the clearance frequently sits with the Chinese ODM. Slide 14 claims the UAE entity "owns the brand, designs, products and application outright" — and says nothing about who would own the 510(k). **That is a question about the core asset, not a footnote.**

**Exposure ranking:**
1. **Cold Sore (highest).** Herpes labialis is a disease; treating it is inherently a medical claim that no reading of FDA's General Wellness policy reaches. A pathway exists — Virulite's De Novo **DEN090012** (2009) created 21 CFR 878.4860, product code OKJ — and follow-ons have cleared against it. SAASH *can* clear. It cannot ship first.
2. **Blemish.** 415nm for blemishes reads as acne vulgaris. Routine, well-trodden, cheap.
3. **AI LED Mask.** Needs OHS clearance plus IEC 62304 software documentation, and carries the FTC "AI" risk.
4. **Cryo (lowest).** "Reduces puffiness, firms" is defensible; "calms irritation" drifts toward a treatment claim. −5°C on periorbital skin adds a thermal-injury file regardless.

**Critical path from a standing start:** CDRH Small Business Determination must be *granted before submission* (30–60 days, fiscal-year specific, nothing in the deck says it is filed) → accredited-lab testing 8–16 weeks (photobiological IEC 62471 and EMC are the queue) → submission prep 4–8 weeks → review, where the MDUFA goal is 90 FDA days but **observed reality is 142–175 calendar days with ~67% receiving Additional Information requests that stop the clock.**

**Total 10–14 months per device, largely parallelisable. Earliest realistic US clearance: Q2–Q3 2027.** The deck's Dec 2026 dates are not achievable; Feb 2027 for the mask is not either. Only May 2027 Cryo survives, and only if it stays cosmetic. **Meanwhile 35% of the raise ($7M) buys inventory against dates regulatory cannot support.**

**The Cold Sore claim is the sharpest live risk.** "The exact protocol tested double-blind against placebo" refers to real science SAASH does not own — Hargate 2006 and Dougal & Lee 2013, both run on the **Virulite** device. Citing another manufacturer's trial is legitimate *inside a 510(k)* as supporting literature. It is not legitimate as consumer advertising: FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance requires competent and reliable evidence **for the advertised product**.

Worst realistic outcome, ordered by likelihood rather than drama: **import refusal / detention without physical examination** under an Import Alert (an uncleared Class II device is adulterated and misbranded) — which strands the inventory — then **Amazon/Sephora delisting**, then a published Warning Letter, then FTC §5, then a piggyback class action. *Import refusal plus delisting is what actually kills the company, because it destroys the 35% inventory position.*

**"Europe and Asia to follow" is expensive.** The Annex XVI transitional extensions apply only to devices lawfully marketed before 22 June 2023 — SAASH gets **no transitional relief**, so full MDR conformity from day one: notified body, ISO 13485, clinical evaluation, PMS/PMCF, EU AR. **12–24 months, €150k–€400k**, notified-body queues binding.

**And UAE domicile hurts rather than helps.** Registration moved to the Emirates Drug Establishment, whose gating requirement is **prior approval in a reference country (US/EU/UK)**. FDA/CE is therefore *upstream* of the home market, not parallel — **the UAE cannot be launched first**, which contradicts the deck's launch sequencing. The Feb 2026 EDE policy also requires more than one appointed agent per product, cutting directly against the "own the customer, single counterparty" thesis on slide 6.

## 5. WHAT THE ROOM WILL MISS

Everyone litigates CAC, margin and FDA. These are the ones that go unasked:

1. **There is no financial model in this deck.** No revenue forecast, no burn, no runway, no valuation, no cap table, no milestone map. Percentages of a pie with no timeline are not a use of proceeds.
2. **The data asset has an exact settled precedent — against it.** Johnson & Johnson's **Neutrogena Skin360 AI skin-analysis app: $4.7M BIPA class settlement, preliminary approval February 2026.** Same category, same claim structure. Add GDPR Art. 9 (biometric/health), **no EU adequacy decision for the UAE** (SCCs on every EU record), UAE PDPL, and Art. 17 erasure rights that make the "compounding record" **deletable on demand**. Slide 9 calls the record "the asset the hardware exists to create." It is at least equally a liability, and it is uninsured and unmentioned.
3. **The cash cycle, not the burn, is the binding constraint.** 30% deposit at PO → 45–60 days production → 30–45 days sea → 14 days customs → 90–180 days sell-through → 30-day returns = **230–300 days, roughly one inventory turn per year.** Of the $20M, **only the $6M marketing line converts into demand.** At realistic CAC that buys $5–8M of revenue and $4–6M of gross profit — less than the marketing that bought it.
4. **The entity is the deal risk.** "A single UAE entity" names no ADGM or DIFC. On mainland UAE, liquidation preference, anti-dilution and drag remain largely untested — which is exactly why funds insist on DIFC/ADGM or a Delaware topco. No US subsidiary for a US-facing DTC business means Wayfair economic-nexus registration across ~45 states, US consumer-protection exposure, and card-acquiring risk from a UAE merchant of record. A US strategic acquirer will demand a re-domiciliation that is a 6–12 month, tax-triggering exercise.
5. **Safety and insurance are absent entirely.** No IEC 62471 photobiological risk-group classification for 213 diodes and 415nm near the eyes, no IEC 60601/62368, no UN38.3 lithium shipping, no product liability cover named. One recall ends this brand.
6. **Governance concentration is total.** Two co-founders own the entire company with no third director, no independent chair, no tiebreak. (Public reporting also indicates the two founders are married; treated here strictly as a governance-concentration fact, not a comment on private matters.) A single deadlock has no resolution mechanism.
7. **Returns are mis-modelled.** A 10–12% provision applied as a margin haircut ignores that a used, skin-contact, battery-powered returned device is not resaleable — it is a near-full COGS write-off plus reverse logistics.
8. **Channel self-contradiction on slide 6.** The deck says 80% of UAE beauty spend is offline and Chalhoub controls ~half the market — then elects DTC-only there. That is a decision to address ~20% of a market of 11M people and call it "the margin market."
9. **The 15-SKU roadmap is a cost, not an asset.** At $400–800k tooling + certification per device, that is $6–12M of NRE and 15 regulatory files against a $2.5M R&D allocation.
10. **Concurrency, not headcount, is the constraint.** Six people running four SKUs × two markets × two (soon three) fulfilment continents, plus an app, plus a formulation line. Hiring to twenty adds management load to founders who have not run an ops org.
11. **The deck's own comp caps its upside.** Slide 4 cites TBTG listing at £300m on £141.0m revenue = **2.1x revenue**. Beauty devices are valued on ~2–4x revenue, not software multiples. A $20M round at a $60–80M post therefore needs ~$40M revenue just to justify entry, and a 10x return needs >$200M revenue — a business larger than TBTG at listing. **The deck chose a comp that proves the category monetises and simultaneously proves the multiple is modest.**

## 6. THE NUMBERS

### 6.1 Break-even, from the deck's own figures

Blemish contribution is $103. But **CAC is paid on returned orders too**, which slide 11 ignores — at the deck's own 10–12% returns provision, $80 becomes ~$90 per *retained* customer, so **order one nets +$13, not +$23**, and fully-loaded break-even CAC is **$92–$103**. The claimed $80 sits only 22% below its own break-even. There is no margin for error anywhere in the number.

| CAC | Order-1 net | Attach needed to break even on 2 orders | LTV/CAC @ 8% attach |
|---|---|---|---|
| $80 (claimed) | +$23 | 0% | 1.68x |

| $200 | −$97 | 19.8% | 0.67x |

*Note the nuance the "CAC kills it" framing misses: at $150 CAC the model does **not** invert — it needs ~10% attach, which is near the realistic base rate. The fatal variable is not CAC alone. It is **CAC × attach**, and the deck discloses neither.*

### 6.3 The one change that fixes the economics

| | As drawn | **Mask-first** |
|---|---|---|
| Median LTV/CAC | 0.84x | **1.33x** |
| P(≥ 1.0x) | 32% | **81%** |
| P(≥ 1.5x) | 7% | **35%** |
| P(≥ 2.0x) | 1.3% | **12%** |

**Lead with the $790 mask and down-sell the $170 devices.** Even at a much higher CAC for a considered $790 purchase ($330 modal vs $170), the entry contribution is 4.5× larger and the down-sell is the easy direction in DTC. This single repositioning moves the probability that a cohort merely returns its cash from **32% to 81%**.

Four independent reasons it is the right call:
1. **The demand is in masks** — SharkNinja alone sold >$70M of LED masks in 2025 against a $35M tracked category the year before (the deck's own slide 4). Blemish has no comparable pull.
2. **Down-sell beats trade-up.** A 4.6× step up is the hardest move in DTC; adding a $170 accessory to a $790 buyer is the easiest.
3. **There is no margin argument for leading cheap** — the deck's own table shows mask contribution (61.9%) *exceeds* Blemish (60.5%).
4. **The fatal internal contradiction:** only the mask has the controller with the sensors. Leading with Blemish means **the data asset — the entire defensibility case of slides 9 and 10 — does not begin until an order two that fewer than 10% of customers ever place.**

## 7. SO — CAN IT WORK?

**Yes, as a business. Probably not as a $20M venture investment at the implied price.**

Separate the two questions, because they have different answers:

**Can SAASH become a real, profitable company?** Roughly **30%**, and materially higher — call it 40–45% — if the ladder is inverted, the ask is cut, and the launch waits for clearance. There is a genuine business here: a well-designed premium device line, honest factory costing, a real tariff position, and a founder who has funded it to production sampling. LYMA does ~$48M of sales and ~$7M EBITDA on direct sales of $2,700–$6,000 devices. That is the achievable shape of success, and it is a good outcome for the founders.

**Can it return a venture fund?** ~**10%**. And at a $20M raise the maths is worse than the 10% suggests, because the deck's own comp values the category at ~2.1x revenue. To make $20M work you need >$200M of revenue — larger than The Beauty Tech Group at listing — from a six-person pre-revenue company with no hardware, software, regulatory or growth leader on the slide.

**The honest read is that this is a $3–6M seed story presented as a $20M Series A** — and the panel converged on that independently from four different directions (round structure, cash cycle, team composition, and the valuation ceiling implied by the deck's own comp). That framing was right, and the panel's contribution is to say *why* with numbers, and to identify that the load-bearing failure is not the $80 CAC in isolation but the **undisclosed 100% attach rate** it is multiplied by.

## 8. WHAT TO DO BEFORE SENDING IT

Ordered by damage prevented per hour of work.

**Fix before the deck leaves the building (days):**
1. **Rewrite or delete slide 8.** Verify every cell against current shipping products, including FOREO BEAR, Nu Skin Prysm iO, Amorepacific Skinsight, and the Korean/Chinese houses. Then make the *stronger* argument: "others have tried contact sensing and retreated — here is what they got wrong and why we won't."
2. **Rewrite the Cold Sore slide.** Remove any implication that SAASH's device was the one tested. Say precisely: "built to the 1072nm protocol validated in [Hargate 2006; Dougal & Lee 2013] on the Virulite device; our own 510(k) is [status]." This is live liability today and costs nothing to fix.
3. **State SAASH's own regulatory status** on every SKU — submitted, in prep, or not started — and who will own the clearance. Silence here reads as concealment even when it isn't.
4. **Label slide 11's attach rate.** Show it at 5%, 10% and 25%, with the CAC sensitivity beside it. A deck that shows its own downside is *more* fundable, not less. This single change converts the deck's biggest vulnerability into a credibility asset.
5. **Explain the margin instead of asserting it.** "We price at $790 a device that costs what a $450 device costs, because [reason]" beats "78–83%" against listed comps every time.
6. **Say what the sebum sensor physically is.** If it needs a consumable, that is *good news* — say so, and put it in the model as recurring revenue.

**Fix before the raise (weeks):**
7. **Invert the price ladder** — lead with the mask. §6.3.
8. **Cut the ask to $4–6M**, one market, two SKUs, tranched on milestones. Take super-pro-rata rights so the $20M becomes reachable at the A, on evidence.
9. **Re-domicile or add a topco** in ADGM/DIFC or Delaware. Without enforceable preference, sophisticated funds cannot invest regardless of the product.
10. **Hire the regulatory/hardware lead before the commercial operator.** A failed or delayed 510(k) is terminal; a slow first sales quarter is not. The deck's stated first hire is the wrong one.
11. **Add a financial model.** Forecast, burn, runway, volume assumptions, cap table.
12. **Spend $50–100k proving CAC in one market before raising on it.** This is the highest-return money the company can spend. A real number from 2,000 real orders is worth more than the entire rest of the deck.

**The strongest version of this pitch:** *one market, one device — the mask — a real CAC number from ninety days of live spend, a filed 510(k), and a $5M ask.* That deck raises. This one gets meetings and then loses them in the room.

## 10. CONVERGENCES AND DISSENT

**Where sealed agents independently agreed** (strongest signals — different evidence, same conclusion):

**Where the panel dissented from the prevailing critique:**
- The manufacturing agent **rejected** the claim that the margin is implausible or the costing understated. $135 at 2,000 MOQ is honest; the tariff work is exact. The margin question is real but it is a *pricing* question, not a *costing* question. Attacking the cost line in a meeting would be a mistake — the founders can defend it, and defending it well would let them dismiss the more serious objections by association.
